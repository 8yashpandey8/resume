Sakshi grew up in a small town where everybody believed life should follow a fixed pattern:
study, find stability, settle quietly.

But Sakshi wanted more than a predictable life.

She spent most evenings sitting near the railway station, watching trains leave for cities she had never seen. Every train felt like a reminder that the world was much larger than the limits people accepted around her.

One day she found an old second-hand copy of a travel journal written by a photographer who had spent years documenting forgotten villages across India. The stories inside the book changed her completely.

The writer described how fear keeps people trapped long after opportunities appear.

That sentence stayed in her mind for months.

Without telling many people, Sakshi started learning photography using an old borrowed camera. She failed repeatedly in the beginning. Her pictures were poorly framed, blurry, and ordinary.

But she continued.

Years later she finally travelled alone for the first time to the mountains of Himachal. During that journey she met strangers with completely different lives:
a teacher who had left corporate life,
a painter who sold art on roadsides,
and a man who had spent ten years rebuilding abandoned schools.

For the first time she understood that success was not a single definition.

By the time she returned home, she was no longer searching for permission to chase her dreams.

Like many classic literary protagonists, Sakshi’s journey was never really about photography.

It was about discovering that fear disappears only after movement begins.
